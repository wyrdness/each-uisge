## Overview

The each-uisge ([ɛxˈɯʃkʲə], 'water horse') is a malevolent water spirit of Scottish Highland folklore, with a close Irish cognate (each-uisce / aughisky) and Manx cognate (cabyll-ushtey). Folklorist Katharine Briggs called it 'perhaps the fiercest and most dangerous of all the water-horses'. It is a shapeshifter, appearing most often as a fine horse or pony but also as a handsome young man or, in some accounts, an enormous bird. When mounted in horse form, the rider is initially safe; once the creature reaches water, its skin turns adhesive and it plunges to the deepest part of the loch, drowning the rider and devouring the body — except, traditionally, the liver, which floats ashore and identifies the cause of death. Unlike the kelpie, which haunts streams and rivers, the each-uisge frequents sea lochs and large freshwater lochs of the Highlands and Hebrides. The creature is documented in 19th-century collections by John Gregorson Campbell and J. F. Campbell, and synthesised in modern scholarship by Katharine Briggs.

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Otherworldly water spirit** _(Highland Gaelic tradition, Katharine Briggs)_

The each-uisge is a malevolent water spirit of the Celtic Otherworld, occupying the same imaginative niche as the kelpie but in larger bodies of water.

### Skeptical

**Cautionary folklore around drowning** _(Modern Scottish folkloristics)_

Each-uisge tales function as a memetic warning system around lochs and sea lochs where drowning was the principal risk; the 'liver on the shore' motif may also encode observation of partially scavenged livestock.
